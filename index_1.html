Murphy’s Law

Anything that can be taxed, will be — even if you haven’t sold it yet.

Economic basics, legal barriers, and political barriers — with Murphy v. United States and Moore v. United States.

I · Basic Economics

Mark-to-Market Economic Basics

  • Accrual tax at a certain point of time more reflective of economic realities.
  • See Haig-Simons definition economists use: income is consumption plus the change in your net worth over the year.

Benefits

  • Corrects distortive treatment of savings accounts versus appreciated assets.
  • Resolves lock-in problem.
  • Simplification: no more distinction between ordinary/capital gain/loss; corporate/shareholder relationships; no depreciation.

Challenges

  • Valuation problem.
  • Liquidity to pay taxes.

II · Legal Barriers

Legal Barriers to Mark-to-Market Taxation

Art. I, § 8 — Direct taxes

Requires direct taxes to be apportioned among the states by population, which is practically impossible for something like a wealth tax, so the fight is over what counts as “direct” (Hylton, Pollock).

Sixteenth Amendment — “Incomes”

Congress may only tax “incomes” without apportionment, so a tax on something that isn’t income, possibly including unrealized gains, loses that exemption.

Fifth Amendment — Due process

Due process bars taxes that are arbitrary or confiscatory, such as attributing income to a taxpayer with no real connection to it.

II · Legal Barriers · Case Study

Murphy v. United States (9th Cir. 1993)

  • § 1256 requires that commodity futures contracts are to be taxed based upon their dollar value on the last business day of the year.
  • Daily settlement: “The trader receives any gain on the position in cash as a matter of right each trading day.” S. Rep. No. 144, 97th Cong., 1st Sess. 156 (1981).
  • No realization problem under constructive receipt doctrine, since daily profits are deposited in investor’s accounts daily.
  • Failure to withdraw daily gains analogized to failure to withdraw interest payments in bank accounts.
Why Congress enacted § 1256 (per the court): to end the use of futures to defer short-term gain and convert it into long-term gain. What the court reserved: whether Congress could tax gains in capital assets before realization or constructive receipt.

II · Legal Barriers · Case Study

Fuzzy Line

“Of course, today’s gain could be eliminated by tomorrow’s loss, but that would not change the fact that today’s gain was available today. That the investment remains at risk is inconsequential; so do loaned or deposited funds.”Murphy, 992 F.2d at 931

Is this true?

  • Credited interest is permanently yours; nothing in the account’s future can take it back. Credited futures gains are yours only in the sense that you can grab them before the next swing; they effectively remain part of an open bet.
  • Macomber’s concept of gain “severed from the capital” relied on the idea that the taxpayer has secured a gain, not just ridden a value up for now.
  • Taxing year-end market value starts to look like taxing a price at one moment.

Moore v. United States (2024)

Reserved
Majority — Kavanaugh, Roberts, Kagan, Sotomayor, Jackson

Refuse to answer whether realization is required, but hold that the corporation’s realized income, attributed to its shareholders, would satisfy any realization requirement.

Not required
Jackson, concurring

“Realization” is just a function Congress uses for administrative convenience, not constitutionally required.

Likely required
Barrett, joined by Alito, concurring in judgment

Realization is likely required, and it must be realized by the shareholder . . . Moore conceded that attribution can be proper in some circumstances and didn’t contest why here.

Required
Thomas & Gorsuch, dissenting

Realization is constitutionally required, therefore this is not an income tax and must be apportioned.

III · Political Barriers

Political Barriers

  • Accrual taxes appear to provide subsidy to risky assets when compared to tax-free alternatives (Brennan).
  • Example: 40% mark-to-market tax on either gains or losses.

Tax-free

$100 → up 100% in year 1 ($200) → down 50% in year 2 → $100

Post-tax

$100 → 60% of the gain kept after year 1 ($160) → halved ($80) plus tax deduction/refund of 40% of the $80 loss ($32) → $112

Looks like subsidy?

III · Political Barriers

Wrong baseline!!

  • Accrual tax is actually two components: (1) cost to switch to an identical portfolio and (2) ex-post tax at termination at rate contingent on short-term risk-free asset but not on portfolio choices.
  • Accrual quietly shrinks your stock exposure. Because the tax takes 40% of every gain and refunds 40% of every loss, you feel only about 60% of the stock’s swings. A taxed 100% stock position works like a mix of about 60% stock and 40% risk-free asset (the “switched portfolio”), depending on the risk-free rate.
  • So if you invest your share in risky asset and the government’s share at the risk-free rate, you are actually worse off. (Their 40% at 10%) ≈ $120, so tax burden is actually around 7%.
  • Government otherwise investing 40% in treasuries.

III · Political Barriers

Why variation matters

Same pre-tax result, bumpier path: better after-tax result.

The mechanism

Taxing 40% of each gain and refunding 40% of each loss dampens the swings, so volatility erodes less of the compounded return.

The example

+100%, then −50%: untaxed, $100 stays $100. Under accrual it becomes $160, then $112.

Constant yield misses it

It taxes as if growth were smooth, dropping the variance term, so it always taxes volatile assets more heavily than accrual (Props. 2–4).

Source: Brennan, The Structure of Accrual Taxation (draft, Jan. 2025), §§ 1, 4–5
